1. Introduction

    Monitoring structural displacement is crucial for maintaining the long-term safety of bridges, buildings, tunnels, and other
civil infrastructure [1]. Structures in service are continuously subjected to operational loads, environmental changes, and material
aging. These factors can induce displacements, potentially leading to cracks, settlements, or even structural instability. Continuous
monitoring allows for the early detection of abnormal behavior, supports timely intervention, and informs maintenance strategies,
thereby enhancing structural reliability and prolonging service life [2,3].
    Displacement measurement methods can be broadly classified into contact and non-contact types [4]. Contact-based methods
rely on physical sensors attached to the structure to directly or indirectly capture motion information. Commonly used instruments
include accelerometers and global navigation satellite systems (GNSS). Accelerometers infer displacement by double-integrating the

measured acceleration [5,6], enabling effective tracking of dynamic responses but introducing low-frequency drift due to integration
errors. In contrast, GNSS provides absolute displacement measurements and has been widely employed for monitoring long-span
bridges and geotechnical structures [7,8]. However, its resolution and sampling rate are insufficient to capture small-amplitude
vibrations in small-scale structures.
    Non-contact methods, mainly including vision- and radar-based methods, have thus gained increasing attention. Vision-based
methods estimate displacement by tracking the motion of features or patterns across sequential images, converting pixel movements
into physical displacements through camera calibration [9,10]. They enable full-field [11] and multi-directional [12] measurements,
making them effective for global displacement assessment. Despite these advantages, vision-based methods still face challenges
related to environmental interference and computational efficiency. Recent studies have addressed these limitations by introducing
a light field correction model to mitigate modal distortion under complex lighting [13], employing a Binary Decomposition Tree
(BDT) for automated modal separation [14], and using a derivative-based phase optical flow algorithm to overcome computational
bottlenecks in large-scale data processing [15]. These advancements have enhanced the robustness, automation, and efficiency of
vision-based non-contact measurements. Radar-based methods, in contrast, mainly determine displacement from the phase variation
of the back-scattered electromagnetic wave, where the phase change is proportional to the target motion along the radar’s line of
sight (LoS). They offer long-range, all-weather capability and exhibit high resilience to illumination and environmental variations
[16]. These characteristics make radar interferometry particularly attractive for robust displacement monitoring in challenging field
environments.
    Previous studies have widely employed centimeter-wave radars (below 30 GHz) for bridge displacement monitoring due
to their long-range coverage and stable signal propagation [17–19]. Millimeter-wave radars, operating at higher frequencies
(typically 60 or 77 GHz), offer shorter wavelengths, enabling higher displacement resolution and improved sensitivity to small-scale
vibrations [20–23]. In addition, Millimeter-wave systems are compact, lightweight, and energy-efficient, making them well-suited
for continuous and high-precision monitoring of small- and medium-span bridges [24]. The shorter wavelength of millimeter-wave
signals also introduces an inherent challenge: phase wrapping. The measured phase is limited to the [−𝜋, 𝜋] interval, and any
displacement exceeding half a wavelength results in ambiguous measurements. For example, a 77 GHz radar has a half-wavelength
of approximately 0.975 mm, highlighting the necessity of effective phase unwrapping for accurate displacement estimation over the
range of ±0.975 mm.
    Various radar-only phase unwrapping algorithms have been developed to tackle the phase wrapping issue. The Itoh-condition-
based method assumes phase changes between consecutive samples are smaller than 𝜋, and corrects wrapped phases by ±2𝜋
increments. However, this assumption often fails in large displacement monitoring [25]. Xu et al. proposed a modified differentiate-
and-cross-multiply (MDACM) algorithm for continuous wave radars, and it detects and corrects phase jumps by differentiating and
cross-multiplying consecutive phase data [26]. Liu et al. adapted the algorithm for FMCW radars using synthesized I/Q signals [27].
More recently, a Doppler-based iterative phase unwrapping method was proposed for multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) FMCW
radars, enabling reliable point-wise phase and displacement tracking under fast target motion, with low computational complexity
[28]. In addition, some methods incorporate additional sensors to mitigate phase wrapping. For example, Zhang et al. combined two
FMCW radar signals at different frequencies to form a virtual signal with a longer effective wavelength, enhancing phase ambiguity
resolution [29]. The authors previously developed an acceleration-aided algorithm to enable adaptive phase unwrapping [30], and
later developed a multi-target algorithm to handle intermittent radar occlusion [31]. However, all these existing methods treat
phase unwrapping as an independent step and do not directly reduce phase noise. As a result, in situations with strong phase noise,
standalone unwrapping methods may fail or give inaccurate results.
    To address these limitations, this study proposes an acceleration-aided Kalman filtering method for joint phase denoising and
unwrapping in FMCW millimeter-wave radar displacement monitoring. The radar phase is modeled as a discrete-time constant-
acceleration system. Measured acceleration is incorporated in the prediction step to improve dynamic tracking and suppress
high-frequency noise. A predictive phase-correction step aligns incoming wrapped measurements with the predicted phase to
remove phase discontinuities. Recursive Kalman updates then produce a continuous and noise-suppressed phase trajectory, which
is subsequently converted to displacement. The noise parameter of the Kalman filter is optimized by minimizing the energy of
the unwrapped phase over a candidate set. At the same time, a direction conversion factor is calibrated by band-pass filtering
radar- and acceleration-derived phases. The Kalman recursion is also reformulated in a convolutional representation, where each
estimate is expressed as a weighted combination of past corrected measurements and acceleration inputs. This representation allows
determination of the minimum convergence time, ensuring stable phase unwrapping.
    Note that although Kalman filter-based methods for simultaneous phase denoising and unwrapping have been reported in
Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR) [32,33] and optical interferometry [34], these studies mainly focus on leveraging
the spatial continuity of phase for topographic mapping in InSAR or achieving high-precision reconstruction of quasi-static or
slowly varying deformations in optical measurements. In both cases, phase predictions rely primarily on the phase’s own historical
evolution (e.g., assuming a constant-velocity model or smooth transitions), representing a typical single-sensor-driven approach. In
contrast, this study proposes an acceleration-aided sensor fusion framework for high-dynamic structural vibration monitoring using
FMCW radar. The key contributions of this study are as follows: (1) development of an acceleration-aided phase unwrapping and
denoising framework based on Kalman filtering; (2) adaptive tuning of Kalman filter noise parameters for robust phase unwrapping
and denoising; (3) quantitative determination of the minimum convergence time required for the Kalman filter to avoid phase
unwrapping failure; (4) enhanced robustness against phase noise, enabling high-accuracy phase estimation even under strong
measurement noise.

3. Acceleration-aided Kalman filtering for joint phase denoising and unwrapping in FMCW radar-based displacement
monitoring

    To address the limitation of standalone unwrapping methods, this study proposes an acceleration-aided Kalman filtering method
for joint phase denoising and unwrapping in FMCW millimeter-wave radar displacement monitoring. As shown in Fig. 2(a), a
millimeter-wave radar and an accelerometer are collocated in the displacement monitoring location of a target structure. The
accelerometer measured structural acceleration in the actual vibration direction, while the radar tracks nearby stationary targets.
The overall flowchart is shown in Fig. 2(b). The raw phase was first extracted from radar measurements. Then the raw phase
and accelerations are fused by a Kalman filter for joint phase denoising and unwrapping (Section 3.1). The estimated phase can
be further converted to displacement based on Eq. (4). In addition, the noise parameter required in the Kalman filter and the
direction conversion factor needed in Eq. (4) are adaptively calibrated (Section 3.2), and the Kalman recursion is reformulated in a
convolutional representation to determine the minimum convergence time for stable phase unwrapping (Section 3.3). Note that the
calibration is performed only once using data collected over a short period, typically less than 1 min. After this one-time calibration,
the resulting calibrated values are used for continuous real-time monitoring.

3.2. Calibration of noise parameters and conversion factor

    The performance of the Kalman filter is strongly influenced by the selection of its noise parameters, namely the process noise
covariance 𝑄 and the measurement noise covariance 𝑅. The parameter 𝑄 represents the uncertainty in the system model, reflecting
how much the filter trusts the predicted state, while 𝑅 characterizes the uncertainty in the radar phase measurement, indicating
how much the filter relies on the observation. Since the output of the Kalman filter primarily depends on the ratio between 𝑄 and
𝑅 rather than their absolute values, one parameter can be fixed while tuning the other. In this study, 𝑅 is set to 1, and 𝑄 is adjusted
as the variable parameter.
    When 𝑄 is excessively large, the filter places undue confidence in the radar phase measurements, making it difficult to effectively
suppress high-frequency phase noise, which can trigger phase unwrapping errors at individual time steps. Conversely, when 𝑄 is
too small, the filter relies excessively on the prediction model, preventing timely correction of model errors and low-frequency
biases, which may likewise result in phase unwrapping failure. Because Kalman filtering operates in a sequential estimation manner,
such unwrapping errors do not remain localized. Instead, they propagate to subsequent time steps and progressively accumulate,
ultimately manifesting as divergence and long-term drift in the estimated phase. From this perspective, the energy of the unwrapped
phase can be regarded as a unified quantitative indicator of phase unwrapping stability. Both noise-induced unwrapping errors
and drift-induced failures contribute to error propagation and accumulation, which are reflected by a pronounced increase in
the unwrapped phase energy. Therefore, minimizing the unwrapped phase energy is, in essence, equivalent to minimizing the
accumulated estimation error energy in the Kalman filtering process. Based on this, an energy-based noise optimization algorithm
is proposed here. Specifically, the unwrapped radar phase is computed for a range of 𝑄𝑖 , and its energy is evaluated as:

4. Laboratory experiment

4.1. Experimental setup


    A four-story building model was first used to assess the long-distance monitoring capability of the proposed method. As shown
in Fig. 5(a), a uniaxial force-balance accelerometer (EpiSensor ES-U2) and a 77 GHz millimeter-wave radar (TI IWR1642) were
installed on the top floor. The EpiSensor ES-U2 is a high-precision force-balance accelerometer for structural appli- cations, offering
a wide dynamic range of up to 155 dB, a bandwidth from DC to 200 Hz, and a user-selectable full-scale acceleration range of ±0.25 g
to ±4 g with multiple output voltage options. The TI IWR1642 is an integrated FMCW radar operating in the 76–81 GHz band, with
up to 4 GHz instantaneous bandwidth, on-chip DSP and ARM Cortex-R4F microcontroller for signal processing and control, typical
transmit power of 12.5 dBm, and a receive noise figure of approximately 14–15 dB. At the same time, horizontal excitation was
applied via a shaking table. Displacements were recorded by a KAIS KL3-W400 laser displacement sensor with 10 μm resolution
[41], which were converted to phase based on Eq. (4) to serve as ground-truth values. The sensors employed in this experiment are
depicted in Fig. 5(b). Data from the radar, accelerometer, and laser displacement sensor were all sampled at a rate of 100 Hz. The
radar operated with a frequency slope of 15.015 MHz/μs, 256 ADC samples, an ADC rate of 6 MHz, and a chirp duration of 60 ms.
A stationary building located roughly 30–60 m away served as the radar target (Fig. 5(c)). A total of seven tests were conducted
using different excitation signals, i.e., 0.1 Hz, 0.3 Hz, 1 Hz, and sweep (0.5–3 Hz) signals.

4.2.2. Phase estimated using target 1 with a low phase noise level
    Phases were first estimated using the raw phase extracted from Target 1. Figs. 7 and 8 compare the raw phase with the estimated
phase obtained using different methods in Tests 1 and 7, respectively. In addition to the proposed method, two existing phase
unwrapping methods, i.e., the Itoh condition-based method [25] and the existing acceleration-aided method [30], were also applied.
Note that the existing acceleration-based method predicts the current displacement using the previous displacement estimates
and the current acceleration, and converts this prediction into a phase to guide the correction of the raw wrapped phase. This
method relies on the raw radar phase being sufficiently reliable during the unwrapping stage. In contrast, the proposed method
formulates displacement estimation within a unified state-space model using an acceleration-aided Kalman filtering framework.
Phase unwrapping is embedded into the recursive filtering process rather than treated as a standalone step, and is performed jointly
with noise suppression. By adaptively balancing acceleration-based prediction and radar observation at each time step, the proposed
method continuously constrains phase evolution, thereby enabling stable displacement estimation even under strong phase noise.
    Due to the relatively large structural motion, the raw phase directly extracted from the radar data was significantly distorted,
as shown in Figs. 7(a) and 8(a). When the building model was excited at a low frequency of 0.1 Hz, all three methods successfully
unwrapped the phase, and the estimation results showed good agreement with the ground-truth phases (Figs. 7(b)–(d)). However,
under a sweep excitation with frequency varying from 0.5 to 3 Hz, only the proposed method and the existing acceleration-aided
method were able to correctly unwrap the phase (Figs. 8(c)–(d)). The assumption underlying the Itoh condition-based method was
violated under this condition, causing it to fail in phase unwrapping (Figs. 8(b)). Note that many advanced phase unwrapping
techniques are not directly applicable under the system constraints considered in this study. For example, dual-frequency methods
require radar systems operating at multiple carrier frequencies [29], while MIMO-based approaches rely on multi-transmit and
multi-receive antenna configurations [28]. Moreover, Kalman filter-based joint phase denoising and unwrapping methods reported
in InSAR [32,33] and optical interferometry [34] are primarily developed for two-dimensional spatial phase maps and exploit spatial
phase continuity, and are therefore not suitable for one-dimensional, time-sequential radar phase unwrapping under high-dynamic
vibration conditions.

performs phase unwrapping and denoising, its performance does not improve compared with the existing acceleration-aided

4.3.2. Conversion factor estimation results
    To calibrate the direction conversion factor, acceleration was first double integrated to yield displacement, which was then
converted to phase based on Eq. (18). Then, the acceleration- and radar-derived phases were band-pass filtered with a frequency
range of [0.5 Hz, 3 Hz] to eliminate low-frequency drift in the former and suppress high-frequency noise in the latter. Note that
for the accelerometer used in this study, lab-scale tests verified that a lower cut-off frequency of 0.5 Hz is sufficient to suppress
low-frequency drift. Based on the acceleration frequency spectrum, the structure exhibits significant vibration only below 3 Hz, and
thus the upper cut-off frequency is set to 3 Hz. Finally, by linearly fitting the radar-derived unwrapped phase to the acceleration-
based phase, the direction conversion factor can be obtained. Fig. 12 shows estimation results for Targets 1 and 2. The estimated
conversion factors are 1.035 and 0.9975 for Targets 1 and 2, respectively. As shown in the figure, a distinct linear relationship is
observed between the acceleration-based and radar-based displacements, with a 𝑅2 value greater than 0.997.

6. Conclusions


   This study proposes an acceleration-aided Kalman filtering method for joint phase denoising and unwrapping in FMCW radar
displacement monitoring. The main innovations uniqueness: (1) development of an acceleration-aided phase unwrapping and
denoising framework based on Kalman filtering; (2) adaptive tuning of Kalman filter noise parameters for robust phase unwrapping
and denoising; (3) quantitative determination of the minimum convergence time required for the Kalman filter to avoid phase
unwrapping failure; (4) enhanced robustness against phase noise, enabling high-accuracy phase estimation even under strong
measurement noise. Experimental validations on a four-story building model and a 48 m long pedestrian bridge led to the following
conclusions:

    • In laboratory tests, due to large structural motions, the Itoh condition-based phase unwrapping method almost completely
      failed for both low- and high-noise targets, with an overall RMSE of 71.596 rad. The existing acceleration-aided method
      successfully handled large motions and achieved an overall RMSE of 2.003 rad for the low-noise target. However, it was
      susceptible to noise and failed for the high-noise target, with an overall RMSE of 37639.95 rad. The proposed method
      successfully performed phase unwrapping and denoising for both low- and high-noise targets, achieving an overall RMSE
      of 2.057 rad. This demonstrates that the proposed method can simultaneously handle large structural motions and high levels
      of phase noise.
    • In field bridge tests, all three methods successfully unwrapped and estimated the phase of the low-noise target, with an overall
      RMSE less than 0.180 rad. For the high-noise target, the existing acceleration-aided method succeeded in 4 out of 9 tests, while
      the Itoh condition-based method succeeded in 7 out of 9 tests. The proposed method successfully unwrapped and estimated the
      phase in all 9 tests, achieving an overall RMSE of 0.276 rad and demonstrating the strongest robustness against phase noise.
      In addition, even for tests where all methods worked, the proposed method reduced the estimation error by approximately
      11% compared to existing methods.
    • Laboratory experiments also validated the importance of properly selecting the noise parameters and minimum convergence
      time in the Kalman-filter-based phase unwrapping and denoising framework. These selections ensure accurate and stable phase
      unwrapping, even under conditions of large motion and high phase noise.

   Future work will implement the proposed method in a compact sensing system and broader real-world applications to further
enhance its practicality and generalization capabilities.
